Estimating sample size for an RT experiment with permutation tests

How many trials each subject completes changes how many subjects you need. Drag the sliders to watch the noise shrink, and to see why the number of people eventually stops falling.

This page uses a flanker- or Stroop-style reaction-time (RT) experiment to show how to estimate the number of subjects you need with simulation and a permutation test, and why the number of trials per subject changes the answer.

Every parameter here (the RT distribution, the effect size, the between-subject variability) is an assumption of mine, not an estimate from real data. Before using this to plan your own study, replace them with values from pilot data or the literature, and run at least one effect size smaller than you expect.

Keep the decision rule and the effect separate

There is no ready-made power formula for a permutation test. It builds its null distribution by reshuffling labels or signs in the data you already have, and that step never produces data in which an effect really exists. So estimating power splits into two jobs: the permutation test supplies the decision rule that says whether a dataset counts as significant, and a generative model you assume supplies the effect. Permuting data washes the effect out, so permutation cannot be used to create H1 data.

Connect the two, repeat many times, and the share of significant results is the power.

The generative model

Each subject has n trials in each of two conditions (congruent, incongruent). Baseline RT is the same in both conditions, so it cancels in the subtraction and does not affect power; in real data, slower people often show larger effects, a correlation not simulated here. Subject i’s true effect θi is normal with mean δ and standard deviation σb.

Each subject is reduced to one number:

di = RTincongruent, i − RTcongruent, i

After averaging n trials, di is approximately normal and can be written as the true effect plus estimation error:

di ≈ θi + εi, Var(εi) = 2σ2tn

What the permutation test does

The null hypothesis in a paired design is that the distribution of the differences di is symmetric around 0. If that holds, flipping the sign of any subject’s difference leaves the data just as probable. So keep every |di|, choose each subject’s sign at random, recompute the group mean, and repeat B times. The result is the distribution of group means you would see if the null were true.

The p value is the proportion of that distribution whose absolute value is at least the observed one, with 1 added to numerator and denominator so p is never 0.

Sweeping N to get power

Hold every subject at 40 trials per condition, run 1000 simulated experiments for each candidate N, and use true mean effects of 25 ms and 15 ms.

For comparison there is also an analytic approximation that holds when di is roughly normal: the standardized effect below, plugged into the power of a paired t-test (noncentral t distribution). It is not the permutation test itself, but the two agree closely when di is near normal, so it works as a check on the simulation and a fast way to scan many parameter sets.

Why the number of trials matters

The permutation test sees one difference di per subject and does not know how many trials stand behind it. Trial count affects power only through the noise in di. From the model above, the variance of di has two terms:

Var(di) = σ2b + 2σ2tn

The blue term is the real difference between people and does not change with n; the vermilion term is trial noise and shrinks as n grows. The explorer at the top of the page draws exactly these two terms. When n is small the vermilion part dominates and more trials shrink the total variance a lot; once n is large only the blue part is left, dz approaches its ceiling δ / σb, and no number of extra trials can get past it.

Taking δ = 15 ms: 10 trials per condition needs about 72 subjects, 40 trials needs 30, 160 trials needs 20, and even unlimited trials needs 16. At δ = 25 ms the matching numbers are 28, 13, 9 and 8.

When planning, pick a combination of subjects and trials per subject first, then check the table to see whether power is enough. If recruiting is expensive, adding trials can save subjects, but the saving has a floor: 16 and 8 subjects for the two effect sizes. The table comes from the analytic approximation, so before relying on it, run power() from the source file on the combination you plan to use.

In practice

  1. Swap in your own generative model. With pilot data you can bootstrap subjects instead of generating them from a parametric model, then compare with the results here. A large gap means the assumed distribution shape is off. A bootstrap cannot extrapolate to an N larger than the pilot.
  2. Swap in the statistic you will actually use. If your analysis uses a median difference, a trimmed mean, or max-statistic correction, change only the statistic inside perm_p; the rest of the pipeline stays the same. This is the main advantage of simulation over an analytic formula.
  3. Run a range of effect sizes. Effect sizes from pilots are usually inflated. Run at least one shrunken value (for example half to two-thirds of the pilot estimate) and report a range of N.
  4. Watch Monte Carlo error. With power near 0.8, S = 1000 gives a standard error of about 0.013; do not read into differences between neighboring values of N that are smaller than that.
  5. Check B. The smallest possible permutation p value is 1/(B+1). With a stricter α (after multiple-comparison correction, for instance), B must grow with it.

Limits

  • Everything depends on the assumed model: independent trials, approximately normal effects across subjects, and no correlation between baseline RT and effect size. Real RT data often show practice effects, fatigue, and serial dependence, which stop trial noise from shrinking as 1/n.
  • σb = 20 ms is an assumption. The smaller the between-subject variability, the longer extra trials keep paying off; the larger it is, the sooner the ceiling arrives.
  • The permutation unit here is the subject (sign flips on subject-level differences). If your real analysis is trial-level (a mixed-effects model, or permuting condition labels within subjects), the exchangeable unit and the simulation have to be redesigned, and these results do not carry over directly.
  • The analytic approximation is only a check. With very few subjects, clearly skewed differences, or outliers, the power of a permutation test and a t-test can differ, and the simulation should be trusted.
