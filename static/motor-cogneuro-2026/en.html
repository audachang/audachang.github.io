The Spatial Map for Action: PPC

The Posterior Parietal Cortex (PPC) is a hub for cross-modal integration:

  • visual data about the cups’ locations
  • proprioceptive information about arm and hand positions
  • tactile feedback from the fingertips

The Spatial Map for Action: PPC

  • Forming an action-centered representation of the environment
    • coding for potential actions rather than just static object locations
  • a cup at (30, 50, −7) ✗
  • a cup to be grasped ✓
  • turning the visual scene into a map of motor possibilities
    • → affordances

The Plan for Action (1): Premotor Cortex

Premotor Cortex (PMC)

  • uses the spatial map from the PPC to prepare specific movements
  • establishes the correct postural set for the body and shoulders

The Plan for Action (2): Supplementary Motor Area

Supplementary Motor Area (SMA)

  • crucial for planning and organizing complex, well-learned, and bimanual sequences
  • Selection of action sequences
  • Coordinating bilateral effectors
  • active even during mental rehearsal of a sequence, not just during physical performance

Motor Execution: The Primary Motor Cortex (M1)

M1 as the motor “launch pad”

  • translating the abstract plan from the PMC and SMA into specific motor commands
    • force
    • direction

M1: Population Coding and Real-time Correction

  • Population coding
    • the final movement direction is determined by the combined, weighted activity of a large group of neurons
  • Real-time correction
    • receiving sensory feedback through long-loop reflexes
    • rapid adjustments to unexpected events, like a cup slipping

Hierarchy in the Motor Neural System

  • Descending systems (upper motor neurons)
    • Motor cortex: planning, initiating, directing voluntary movements
    • Brainstem centers: basic movements, postural control
  • Basal ganglia: gating proper initiation of movement
  • Cerebellum: sensory motor coordination of ongoing movement
  • Spinal cord & brainstem circuits
    • Local circuit neurons → motor neuron pools → skeletal muscles

Why Exercise is Beneficial for the Brain? (1) Short-term

Balancing Neurotransmitters

  • Norepinephrine → Attentional Control
  • Serotonin → Emotional Regulation
  • Dopamine → Association between reward and learning

Why Exercise is Beneficial for the Brain? (2) Long-term

Enhancing the production of neurotrophins for plasticity

  • BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) → forming neurons and synapses
  • IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1) → facilitating BDNF
  • VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) → forming new capillaries
  • FGF-2 (fibroblast growth factor 2) → strengthening new neurons

BDNF is a member of the neurotrophin family. Exercise increases BDNF secretion; BDNF secreted by contracting muscle also plays a role in muscle repair and regeneration.
